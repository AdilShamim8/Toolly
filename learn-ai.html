1 Stage 1 — The Basics
Weeks 1–2 · understand what AI is and start using it
Lesson 1 · Start here 10 min

What AI actually is — in plain language

Today's AI tools you read about — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and hundreds of others — are built on large language models (LLMs): systems trained on enormous amounts of text so they can predict what words plausibly come next. That's the whole trick. It makes them excellent at drafting, summarising, rephrasing and explaining — and it explains their one big weakness: they can sound confident while being wrong, a habit called “hallucinating.”

Three mental models that make everything else click:

  • It's a brilliant intern, not an oracle. Fast, tireless, well-read — but new on the job. You give context and check the work.
  • It predicts, it doesn't know. Useful output comes from good input; vague question, generic answer.
  • It has no memory of you between chats unless a feature says otherwise. Each conversation starts fresh.

The three things a beginner actually needs

  1. An assistant — one chatbot you open daily (next lesson helps you pick).
  2. A method — the four-part way of asking that gets good results (Lesson 4).
  3. A habit of checking — treating output as a first draft you verify (Lesson 5).
Try it now

Open any AI chatbot and ask: “Explain how you work to a complete beginner, in 5 short bullets, no jargon.” Then ask a follow-up: “Where can you go wrong?” Notice how the follow-up builds on the first answer — that back-and-forth is the core skill of this whole roadmap.

  • I can explain what an LLM does in one sentence
  • I understand why confident-sounding answers can be wrong
  • I've had my first two-message conversation with an AI

Lesson 3 · First win 10 min

Your first useful AI task

The mistake beginners make is asking abstract things (“tell me about marketing”) instead of handing over a real job. The fastest way to feel the value is to give AI one small task you already have lying around, then judge the result yourself.

  1. Pick one real, small task. A messy email, meeting notes, a paragraph that won't behave. Not “learn AI” — one concrete job.
  2. Write a clear request. Paste the material and say what you want: shorter, friendlier, three bullets, a reply.
  3. Check the result against what you know. Since it's your own material, you can instantly spot wrong or off-tone bits.
  4. Refine with one follow-up. “Shorter,” “more formal,” “keep the deadline but drop the apology.” Follow-ups are half the skill.

Example · a real first task

“Here are my rough notes for tomorrow's team update: [paste]. Turn them into 4 short bullets in a positive but honest tone. Flag anything that looks unclear so I can fix my notes.”

Try it now

Find one real email in your inbox that needs a reply today. Paste the thread into your assistant and ask: “Draft a short, polite reply that answers the questions and moves this forward.” Edit the draft before sending — the edit is where your judgment stays in charge.

  • I've completed one real task end-to-end with AI
  • I used at least one follow-up to refine
  • I understand the pattern: task → request → check → refine
2 Stage 2 — Daily Use
Weeks 3–4 · make AI reliable and safe in your routine
Lesson 4 · The core skill 12 min

The four-part prompt method

Almost every good prompt contains the same four ingredients. Learn them once and they work in every chatbot, image generator, or AI feature inside an app you'll ever meet:

  1. Role — who should the AI be? “You're a careful editor…” focuses the tone and vocabulary.
  2. Task — the verb. Draft, summarise, compare, rewrite, list. One main verb per prompt beats three vague ones.
  3. Context — what it needs to know: audience, purpose, length, what to avoid. This is the part beginners skip and experts nail.
  4. Format — what the result should look like: bullets, table, 100 words, plain language.

Before

“Write something about our new pricing.”

After — same request, four parts in

“You're a customer-friendly product marketer (role). Write a 120-word announcement (task) of our new free tier for existing users, who were worried prices would rise (context). Warm, no hype, end with one clear next step (format).”

Then keep the conversation going: “make it 80 words,” “more concrete,” “give me two subject lines.” A prompt isn't a form you fill once — it's a conversation you steer.

Try it now

Take a vague request you'd genuinely like answered this week and rewrite it with all four parts. Send it, then send one refinement. Compare the first answer to the refined one — that gap is what the method buys you.

  • I can name the four parts without looking
  • I've rewritten one vague request into a four-part prompt
  • I've seen the improvement with my own eyes
Lesson 5 · Good habits 9 min

Checking an AI answer

Language models answer with plausibility, not certainty. For low-stakes work (a draft email) that's fine — you'd proofread anyway. For anything you'll sign, send to a client, or publish, build this simple habit:

  1. Ask for its sources first. “Which claims here would need verifying, and where would you check them?” A model that flags its own weak spots is far more useful than one that just writes.
  2. Verify anything checkable — numbers, dates, names, quotes, URLs, prices — in the source of record, not another chatbot.
  3. Treat “facts” about people and companies as drafts. Models can mix up entities with similar names.
  4. Trust your expertise loudest. In your own field you are the fact-checker. Where you're not the expert, keep the stakes low or verify externally.

A related habit: keep a small file of prompts that worked well for you. It becomes your personal playbook, and it's the difference between “using AI” and “being good at using AI.”

Try it now

Ask your assistant about a topic you know well — your hobby, your industry, your neighbourhood. Find one thing it gets subtly wrong. That experience teaches more about AI's limits than any article.

  • I ask AI to flag which claims need checking
  • I verify numbers, names and quotes before relying on them
  • I've caught at least one AI mistake myself
Lesson 6 · Good habits 8 min

Know what you share with AI

Every prompt you type is data leaving your device. For public knowledge (“explain inflation simply”) that's harmless. For work and personal material, a one-minute check is worth it:

  • The share test: would you be comfortable if this text appeared on a company noticeboard? If not, anonymise it — swap real names for A/B, cut figures that identify a client, describe rather than paste.
  • Never paste: passwords, full ID numbers, credit cards, medical records, anything under a confidentiality agreement you're not sure about.
  • Know where things live. Most chatbots keep your conversation history in your account; providers may use interactions to improve models unless you turn that off in settings. Check the privacy controls once, then relax.
  • Browser tools are different: sites like Toolly keep your saved tools and settings in your browser's local storage — on your device, not on a server. It never leaves unless you share it.

Anonymising is a skill, not an obstacle. “A customer with an overdue invoice of about €X” gets 95% of the help with none of the exposure.

Try it now

Take a work document you'd like help with and rewrite one paragraph so nothing in it identifies the client, the amounts, or the people. Notice how little usefulness you lose.

  • I do the share test before pasting anything sensitive
  • I've anonymised one real document successfully
  • I know where my chat history and local settings live
Lesson 7 · Routine 10 min

Putting AI into your day

Sporadic use fades; anchored habits stick. Attach AI to something you already do — the technique habit-builders call stacking. Pick one anchor to start:

  • Morning (10 min): “Here's my day: [calendar or list]. Tell me what to do first and what could slip. Ask me anything unclear.” A five-minute plan beats a perfect one you never make.
  • Inbox triage: paste a long thread: “Summarise in 3 bullets, list what's actually asked of me, draft a reply.”
  • End of day (5 min): “Turn these rough notes into tomorrow's to-do list, grouped by morning/afternoon.”
  • Weekly review: “Here's what happened this week: [notes]. What patterns should I look at for next week?”

After two weeks of one anchor, a second comes naturally. Also worth knowing: modern assistants can now work on long documents and whole folders — meeting transcripts, manuals, research PDFs — so “summarise this 80-page report” is a normal Tuesday request, not a party trick.

Try it now

Run the morning version tomorrow: paste your task list and ask for a prioritised plan with one question about whatever's ambiguous. Do it for five weekdays, then decide if it earns a permanent slot in your routine.

  • I've chosen one daily anchor for AI
  • I've run it at least five times
  • I can tell whether it's paying for its minutes
3 Stage 3 — Beyond Text
Weeks 5–6 · images, voice, and smarter search
Lesson 8 · Create 10 min

Making images and voice

Generative image tools have two jobs for beginners: concepting (fast drafts of an idea before you invest in the real thing) and everyday visuals (a quick illustration, a social post, a mood board). The same four-part method applies — just swap “task” for subject and “format” for style:

Anatomy of an image prompt

Subject: a small coffee shop on a rainy morning · Style: warm watercolour illustration · Details: soft light, two customers, no text in the image · Format: wide banner, muted colours

  1. Describe like a director — subject, setting, mood, lighting. Words like “soft,” “flat,” “cinematic” change everything.
  2. Say what to exclude — “no text,” “no logos.” Negative instructions matter.
  3. Iterate, don't restart — “same image, warmer light, fewer people” keeps what worked.

Voice and music tools work the same way: describe the voice (“calm narrator, slight warmth, medium pace”) or the mood (“lo-fi study beat, 90 seconds”) and iterate. For anything reaching the public, keep one rule in mind: be transparent when content is AI-generated, and check the tool's licence for commercial use before you sell or publish.

Find image, video, audio and design tools in the directory's create categories.

Try it now

Ask an image generator for three very different versions of one thing you need — same subject, three styles (photo, flat illustration, watercolour). Study which words changed which result. That's the entire skill, condensed.

  • I've generated images with a structured prompt
  • I've used follow-ups to refine rather than restart
  • I know to check licences before commercial use
4 Stage 4 — Automation
Weeks 7–8 · let AI do the boring parts on its own
Lesson 9 · Level up 12 min

Your first automation

Chats are you-doing-the-work. Automation is the boring parts happening without you. The state of the art has moved fast: agentic AI — assistants that can take a goal, break it into steps, use tools, and check their own work — is now built into mainstream products, and no-code platforms have made wiring these flows a beginner skill.

The ladder, in order of difficulty:

  1. Saved prompts (minutes) — keep your best prompts in a note; paste and adapt. You already do this if you did Lesson 5's playbook.
  2. Custom assistants/GPTs (under an hour) — most big chatbots let you save a persona plus instructions: “my weekly-report helper: tone, format, my role.” Then it's one click each week.
  3. Rules-based flows (an evening) — no-code tools connect apps: “new form response → draft a personalised reply → log it to a sheet.” This is where automation starts feeling like magic.
  4. AI agents (when you're ready) — give a goal, not steps: “research these five competitors and produce a comparison table with sources.” Powerful, and exactly where the checking habits from Lesson 5 matter most.

Two rules keep automations safe and useful: start with a draft, not an action (let it prepare the email, not send it), and watch each new flow run three times before you trust it.

Ongoing

Where to go next

You now have the complete beginner's toolkit: a daily assistant, a prompt method, checking habits, privacy instincts, and your first automation. Everything from here is depth in the direction you actually need. Three sustainable habits beat any course:

  • One new thing a month. A feature you haven't tried, a category in the directory you've never opened. Small, curious, deliberate.
  • Keep your playbook alive. Prompts that worked, prompts that flopped. Six months of that file is a personal superpower.
  • Share what you learn. Teaching a colleague the four-part method is the fastest way to find the gaps in your own.

A note on staying current without drowning: the models improve constantly, but the skills in this roadmap barely age — asking well, checking carefully, sharing safely, automating thoughtfully. Master those and every new tool that arrives is a five-minute adaptation, not a fresh learning curve.
